Subgroup power, dug back up.

Hayward, Kent, Vijan and Hofer simulated thousands of clinical trials in 2006 and released no code. In November 2026 we rebuilt the simulation from the paper and reran every setting. Of 19 results we tried, 8 agree, 9 partly agree and 2 differ. The main claim came back.

What stayed buried

  • Some powers. Where the heterogeneity is large our powers run higher than the paper's: Table 2 5 of 9, Table 3 7 of 18, Table 5 2 of 8 within tolerance. No tested reading of the paper closes the gap.
  • Table 1's rates. Its CER, RRR and NNT columns follow from no population of the stated risk factors, nor from each other.

What we matched

What the paper does not say, and what we chose.

read from figure
The curve's CER range, 0.016 to 0.075, is read from the published curve's ends. The score is over the drawn curve only; a range 0.001 shorter at either end removes about 7 px of curve and changes neither share by more than 2%.
paper
Risk factors act on the odds scale (odds ratios), for every table. Tables 2 and 3 and Fig. 1B say odds ratio; Table 4, Table 5 and the text say relative risk. Six RFs of relative risk 3.0 (Table 3) would give risks above 1, and Tables 2-5 point back to Table 1's population. On the relative-risk scale 18 of 59 printed powers and AUROCs come back, against 28 of 63 on the odds scale (comparison.json, hypotheses.rr_scale).
paper
Treated risk EER = CER x .5 + .003 x 5, as printed in Fig. 1A (harm counted as an outcome, added to the halved risk). Outcome and complication as independent events, EER = 1 - (1 - .5 CER)(1 - .003)^5, gives 28 of 63 (hypotheses.harm_independent).
our choice
Each trial draws 4,400 people per arm with replacement from an infinite population (exact pattern shares); the interaction is tested with a Wald test; 20,000 trials per setting, our own seeds. A likelihood-ratio test gives 28 of 63; 2,000 trials, as in the paper, 34. A second run with another seed agrees with the first within the tolerance in 41 of 41 powers.
our choice
Comparison tolerance for a power: half a printed unit plus 3 combined Monte Carlo standard errors, the paper's (2,000 trials) and ours (20,000), fixed before the comparison. Other values at their printed precision. With the paper's 2,000 trials on our side too the tolerance is wider and 34 of 63 come back.
found by search
The AUROC of a risk index is that of the index for the 5-year outcome over both arms of the trial (control and treated people together), from the population. The paper says only 'from a logistic regression model'. Chosen after comparing with the control arm alone. The control arm alone gives AUROCs 0.01 to 0.07 higher; 22 of 63 printed values come back with it (hypotheses.auroc_control_arm).
paper
Fig. 1B: four RFs of OR 2 and prevalence 25%; the baseline risk (no RF) is set so the population's average CER is 1.5%, 3%, 6% or 9%; the one-RF analysis tests one of the four. The panel names the four RFs, their OR and prevalence, and the average CERs; how the average was reached is ours (baseline CER 0.625%, 1.272%, 2.629%, 4.073%).
paper
In Table 2 the RF analysed is one of six; the other five have prevalence 25% and OR 2 (the table's footnote). Giving the other five the analysed RF's OR changes Table 2 to 5 of 9 within (hypotheses.table2_others_same_or).
our choice
In Table 3 the population keeps six RFs: the index's k RFs have the column's OR and the other 6 - k have OR 2, so the 1-RF column is Table 2's 25% column, as printed. The paper does not say. A population with only the index's k RFs gives 10 of 18 within (hypotheses.table3_only_index_rfs).

We rebuilt a lost power simulation from its paper and ran every setting again.
